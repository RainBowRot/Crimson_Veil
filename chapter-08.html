Chapter VIII

When Dawn Forgets

He woke because some old part of him still expected light.

The windows remained black. Seraphine watched him from the chair, already dressed in the same black lace high-collar gown, red eyes calm. The tattoos across his chest looked darker, as if the night had claimed the ink too.

Cassian

“You were waiting for the sun,” she said.

“How long?”

“Long enough that you should have noticed.” She came to him, gloved fingers tracing the ink at his collarbone. “The hotel does not keep hours, Cassian. It keeps guests.”

He caught her wrist. Not gently.

“You told me one night. You let it steal the morning without asking me. That is not your rule. That is the house using your mouth.”

The red in her eyes flared, then banked. She could have broken his grip. She did not. She let the bruise of his fingers be a fact.

Seraphine

“Yes,” she said. “I am guilty of that. I offered you a night because nights are the only currency I am allowed to spend. Dawn is the house’s. I have been a coward about the difference. I wanted you in my dark before you had enough information to hate me correctly.”

He did not let go. “If I want to stay past being a guest, I stay as a man who can still want morning. Not as a blank in a ledger.”

Her voice went thin. “Then you stop asking the dark for permission, and you start asking me to commit treason against my own walls. I can offer you what the night offered me. Not death. The dawn will forget you the way it forgot me. The house will try to file you. I will have to stand between. I have failed that stand before.”

He saw it then: she was not only the predator. She was the oldest kept thing in the building. Continuity. A title the cliffs had instead of a widow.

He loosened his hand. He kissed the lace at her pulse, an apology that did not unmake the anger.

“Then we go to the garden,” he said, “and you tell me the whole price where the roses can hear it. If I say yes after that, it is mine.”

Seraphine closed her eyes. For a woman who did not blink enough, it looked like prayer.

“If you say no,” she said, “I will fight the road for you. I do not promise I will win. I promise I will be the one fighting, not the one smiling while the corridor eats your name.”
