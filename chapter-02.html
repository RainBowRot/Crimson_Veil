Chapter II

The Invitation

Seraphine

She had watched him fail the road three times before she bothered to become a doorway.

The Obsidian chose by pulse. Seraphine chose more slowly. She had learned the difference the hard way: the house could keep a body in a quiet room for a century and call it hospitality. She wanted, against her own contract, a guest who still argued.

He argued with his feet. That was something.

Cassian

She appeared in the library as if the shadows had simply chosen to become a woman.

Seraphine. Platinum hair swept into an updo that still looked as if a hand had been in it. Skin so pale it seemed to hold moonlight instead of blood. Red eyes that did not blink often enough. Dark burgundy mouth. A black lace high-collar Victorian gown, sheer sleeves showing the line of her arms, the kind of dress that had outlived the people who first feared it.

She held a single dark rose. Not a welcome. A sample.

“You keep trying to leave,” she said, voice low enough that the shelves leaned in. “The road has a way of bringing you back.”

Cassian did not ask how she knew. He asked the ruder thing.

“Do you stop them, or does the building?”

A pause. The candles considered it.

“The building is older than my manners,” Seraphine said. “I am only its continuity. I offer one night. The house offers forever, and it does not always wait to be asked.”

He should have found that ugly. He found it honest, which was worse.

She crossed the carpet without sound and set the rose in his hand. The stem was thornless, which felt like a lie dressed as courtesy. Up close she smelled of cold iron and crushed petals. His tattoos itched, the way they did before a sitting, when the needle was about to write something that would not come off.

“I don’t stay,” he said.

Her smile was slow. “Then you and I have that in common. I have not left these cliffs since the daylight stopped recording me. And still I am here, practicing the art of remaining. You may find it contagious.”

He closed his fingers on the rose. It did not bleed. He almost wished it would. Blood at least admitted what it was doing.
