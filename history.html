Eternal Night
Contents Warnings Book I

Appendix

Found History

What the hotel remembers of itself.

Origins

The Obsidian Hotel was not founded so much as it arrived. Local Cornish records treat it the way they treat certain standing stones and fog banks: present, old, and best left alone.

Long before it had a name travelers could speak, the site was a private manor belonging to a coastal family whose bloodline thinned in the early 1800s. The last heir—remembered only as the Lady of the Cliff—did not die in any documented way. She simply stopped being seen in daylight. The house remained lit. Servants left or stayed according to their own quiet agreements. Fog thickened around the headland until the road itself seemed to forget how to end anywhere else.

By the mid-Victorian years the manor had become known, among those who needed to know, as a place that accepted certain guests without asking for luggage. It acquired the name Obsidian the way a bruise acquires color—slowly, and only after the wound had already settled.

Nature of the Place

The Obsidian does not keep ordinary hours. Clocks inside it are decorative. Windows show night more often than they should. Guests who are meant to find it do; others drive past the same turn three times and never see the gates. The building appears to grow rather than be maintained: black stone, tall candlelit windows, a constant veil of sea fog that never fully lifts. Rooms rearrange their emphasis according to who is staying. Some corridors lead to the garden of purple roses. Some lead only back to the same landing.

Seraphine is not the hotel’s owner in any legal sense. She is its continuity. Whether she was the original Lady, or the first guest who stayed long enough to become the place’s memory, is a distinction the Obsidian itself does not bother to make.

Fragmentary Patterns

After certain storms, a small number of survivors washed up near the cliffs and were never recorded in the ledgers of nearby villages. The hotel’s guest books from those decades are incomplete—or were never meant to be complete.

Locals say the approach road has a habit of bringing people back. Cassian’s experience is not unique; it is simply the most recent clear example.

On ordinary nights the sun still rises beyond the fog. On nights when the hotel decides a guest is finished with mornings, dawn simply fails to arrive for them. This is not a spell so much as a change of jurisdiction.

The Bargain

The Obsidian Hotel does not feed in the crude sense. It keeps. It offers a particular contract: the surrender of the measured day in exchange for a night that does not end and a belonging that does not require explanation. Seraphine’s invitation of “one night” is the hotel’s oldest agreement, restated in human language — and, in the events of this edition, the first agreement she breaks on purpose.

By the time Cassian arrives, the hotel has already chosen him the way it chooses its rare permanent guests—by pulse, by the refusal to leave when leaving is still possible. Seraphine chooses more slowly. The quarrel of the book is that those two choosings are not the same. The house wants a quiet. She wants a man who argues. The ledger room on the locked floor is where the hotel files the difference.

Seraphine’s private rule is that she does not drink what has not been offered. The house has no such rule. It drinks hours. It has drunk guests she meant only to shelter. That failure is the wound she wears as manners.

Present

After the events recorded in these pages, the Obsidian no longer waits for travelers with a mouth. It has keepers. The lights still burn. The fog still seals the road. Guests who find the gates are given weather, not forever. The ballroom is sometimes occupied by two figures who count nothing — except each other, and the doors.

The hotel’s history is therefore unfinished in the ordinary sense. It does not accumulate years so much as it accumulates nights — and, now, the people willing to stay inside them without being filed.
